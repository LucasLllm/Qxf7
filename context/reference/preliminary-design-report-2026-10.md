## Project Goal

**Our goal is to move medical supplies and lab samples between remote Canadian communities and the hospitals that serve them faster and more reliably, all year round, winter included.**

### The problem

Across northern and coastal Canada, many small communities have no year-round road to a hospital. Indigenous Services Canada alone staffs nurses in 50 remote and isolated First Nations communities: 24 in Ontario, 21 in Manitoba, 4 in Alberta and 1 in Quebec (2022 figures). Quebec has other isolated communities too, such as most Lower North Shore villages beyond the end of Route 138, Entry Island in the Magdalen Islands, and Nunavik's 14 Inuit villages, only two of which have a hospital. In most of these places, everyday care comes from a small clinic run mainly by nurses, and the nearest hospital and laboratory are a flight or a boat ride away.

These clinics depend on deliveries in both directions: blood, urine and swab samples go out for testing, and prescriptions, vaccines and supplies come in. Many of these loads are small, a few tubes or a box of pills, but they are time-sensitive. For example, Montreal's Optilab Montréal–CUSM laboratory requires outside clients' blood count tubes to arrive within 3 hours of collection and uncentrifuged chemistry tubes within 1 hour, and it rejects late samples. A remote clinic can spin and refrigerate samples to buy time, but a delay of a day or more still limits which tests can be done, and a rejected sample means taking blood from the patient again.

These small loads have no dedicated transport. They ride on whatever carries people and freight: scheduled flights, helicopters, ferries and barges, and in winter, ice roads and snowmobile trails. Each stops for its own reasons. Aircraft are grounded by fog, wind and storms. Boats stop for ice, storms and maintenance. Ice roads open only once the ice is thick enough and close when it softens. Chartering an aircraft for one box of samples is usually too expensive, so the box waits for the next scheduled trip.

These links are also becoming less reliable. Canada relies on about 8,000 km of official winter roads, and researchers at Ouranos estimate that the length of road usable in winter could fall by 30 to 40% in the northern territories and by more than 60% in Manitoba and Ontario. Nearly 70 northern communities are fly-in only for most of the year and depend on a winter road open for six to eight weeks to bring in their year's food, fuel and medical supplies.

**In short, time-sensitive medical deliveries to these communities ride on passenger and freight transport that can stop for days or weeks at a time, most often in winter. The two case studies below show what this looks like in practice.**

**These routes are examples, not final plans. Assessing real needs with the people involved is an essential next step. We plan to speak with community representatives, clinic staff, laboratory staff and existing transport providers to understand actual delivery volumes, package sizes, temperature requirements, collection and handoff times, seasonal disruptions and local priorities. These discussions have not yet taken place; our current case studies and demand estimates are preliminary research, not confirmed community requirements. We will use this consultation to assess and refine the proposed service and its operating requirements.**

### Case study 1: Island Lake, Manitoba

Island Lake is a group of four First Nations in northeastern Manitoba: Garden Hill, St. Theresa Point, Wasagamack and Red Sucker Lake. Together they have at least 15,000 people, about the population of Thompson, yet the region has no hospital. Care comes from understaffed nursing stations: in 2022, Garden Hill had four nurses for about 5,000 people, and Wasagamack's station usually has two or three of its five nurses, plus a doctor who flies in once a week. Patients who need a hospital are usually flown to Winnipeg, nearly 500 km away. The region is reached only by air, by boat, and by a winter road open one to two months a year, less in warm winters.

Wasagamack, with about 2,300 people, shows the problem most clearly because it has no airport. The nearest one, at St. Theresa Point, is 10 to 12 km across the lake. From May to October, non-urgent patients cross by boat, and choppy water or bad weather can strand them; urgent cases leave by helicopter, and in winter people cross on the ice road. Wasagamack averages one medevac a day, at $8,000 to $10,000 each, and about 100 people a week cross the lake for medical appointments. Getting a patient to urgent care in Winnipeg takes about five hours. On the day CBC visited, a community health representative was collecting sputum from tuberculosis patients, samples that must go out to a lab.

Winter brings its own risk. On February 6, 2024, with warm weather delaying the 294 km winter road from Berens River, the four chiefs declared a state of emergency. Garden Hill had about a week of fuel left, the communities were waiting on close to 1,000 truckloads of fuel, food, water, building and medical supplies, and the road did not open until the third weekend of February. Manitoba committed in 2024 to building an airport in Wasagamack after about 60 years of requests. Until it opens, every trip out starts with a boat, helicopter or ice-road crossing to a neighbouring community.

#### How a drone would help

A 750 g payload, including insulated packaging, is enough for a typical batch of samples from a nursing station like Wasagamack's: blood tubes, urine containers and tuberculosis sputum samples. On the way back, it can carry a set of prescriptions or small urgent medications such as antibiotics or naloxone. At about 70 km/h, the drone would reach the St. Theresa Point airport in about 10 to 12 minutes, including take-off and landing, and could make the trip several times a day.

The drone would not replace the boat or the helicopter for patients; it would take over the small, time-sensitive loads. Samples could leave as soon as they are collected and make the next scheduled flight out of St. Theresa Point, instead of waiting for a boat or a free helicopter seat. Because it takes off and lands vertically, it needs no runway or dock, and during freeze-up and break-up, when the lake is neither open water nor safe ice, it could still fly when the weather allows.

**Route:** Wasagamack nursing station to the St. Theresa Point airport, about 10 to 12 km across Island Lake.

### Case study 2: Manawan, Quebec

Manawan is an Atikamekw community of about 2,500 people in northern Lanaudière, with no airstrip. Its only road link to the rest of Quebec is the chemin de Manawan, an 86 km forestry road to Saint-Michel-des-Saints. Only the first 8 km are paved; the other 78 km are gravel, with sharp curves, seven one-lane bridges and heavy logging trucks. Front-line care comes from the community's Masko-Siwin health centre. The regional hospital, the Centre hospitalier De Lanaudière in Joliette, is about 190 km away.

The road sets the pace of every medical trip. In 2018, a Saint-Michel-des-Saints paramedic told Radio-Canada that crews drive at 60 km/h even in an emergency to avoid breaking the vehicle. Manawan's ambulance leaves with a patient 300 to 350 times a year, and each trip takes it out of the community for at least four hours. From 2016 to 2020 the road saw about 50 accidents, 20 of them with injuries, and there were fatal crashes in 2021 and 2022. Quebec promised to rebuild it in 2018. With no major work done, the community went to court in October 2025.

Lab samples depend on the same road. The regional laboratory at the Joliette hospital accepts specimens from clinics in its territory only on weekdays between 8:00 and 16:00, and it destroys most outside samples that arrive after hours. For a clinic 190 km away, 78 km of it on gravel, a slow trip or a closed road can mean a lost sample.

#### How a drone would help

Manawan's routine medical traffic is small: a batch of samples from the health centre, or a patient's prescriptions, fits within 750 g including insulated packaging. With a range of about 95 km per battery, the drone could fly from Manawan to the Saint-Michel-des-Saints CLSC (about 70 km), take a fresh battery there, and continue to the Joliette hospital (about 80 km). At about 70 km/h, the 150 km trip would take a little over two hours of flying, with no vehicle or driver on the gravel road.

The main gain is reliability and timing. Because the Joliette laboratory only accepts outside samples on weekdays between 8:00 and 16:00, flights could be scheduled to land inside that window every weekday, whatever the state of the road. On the way, the drone could pick up the Saint-Michel-des-Saints CLSC's own samples, and on the return it could bring prescriptions and small medications to Manawan. It would not replace ambulances; it would take routine loads off the road.

**Route:** Manawan to Saint-Michel-des-Saints CLSC (about 70 km, battery swap) to Joliette hospital (about 80 km).

#### Others already working on this route

Manawan's need is already recognized. In 2023 the band council backed CORRIDAIR, a project of Vertiko Mobilité and Jaunt Air Mobility for a 150 km drone corridor from the Joliette hospital to Manawan, and Quebec granted it up to $2 million in 2024. Deliveries were planned for 2026, but we found no report of flights yet. Our small drone would complement CORRIDAIR, flying the frequent sample runs that don't justify a large cargo drone, in partnership with the community and the CORRIDAIR team.

### Our solution: an electric VTOL fixed-wing drone

Our answer is a VTOL (vertical take-off and landing) fixed-wing drone: a small electric aircraft that takes off and lands straight up like a helicopter, then flies on a wing like an airplane.

#### Why a drone?

The loads in our case studies are light, frequent and urgent: a few tubes of blood, a box of prescriptions. A dedicated plane, boat or truck trip for each one costs far too much, so these loads wait. A drone carries no pilot or driver, so it can fly a small load on its own schedule, as often as needed, for little more than a battery charge and a remote pilot's time. It needs no road, dock or safe ice, so it can keep the link open when those fail. The idea has been tested: in January 2024, the Quebec non-profit ECOGENE-21 and the French company Delair flew an electric VTOL drone more than 150 km on a test range in France, carrying medicines and blood samples.

#### Why a fixed wing instead of a multicopter?

Most drones people know are multicopters, such as quadcopters, which hold themselves up with four or more propellers. Those propellers carry the drone's full weight for the whole flight, which uses a lot of energy and limits most multicopters to short trips. A fixed-wing drone gets its lift from a wing as it moves forward, like an airplane, so its propeller only has to push it ahead. On the same battery, it flies several times farther than a multicopter of similar size, and faster. That matters for routes of 10 to 80 km, and speed helps against strong northern winds.

#### Why VTOL instead of a regular fixed wing?

A regular fixed-wing drone needs a runway, a catapult or a hand launch to take off, and a long open field, a net or a parachute to land. A remote clinic rarely has room for these, especially for landing. A VTOL fixed-wing drone adds lifting propellers used only for take-off and landing. It rises straight up from a small pad, switches to flying on its wing for the trip, and lands vertically at the other end. The lifting motors add weight and drag, but they run for only a minute or two on each leg.

In summary:

|  | **Multicopter** | **Regular fixed wing** | **VTOL fixed wing** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Take-off and landing | Vertical, small pad | Runway or catapult | Vertical, small pad |
| Range and speed | Short and slow | Long and fast | Long and fast |
| Lands at a clinic | Yes | Difficult | Yes |

### Success criterias

Success criterias are the targets QxF7 must meet for the project to succeed. Each one comes from our goal: carrying what remote clinics actually send, over the distances that matter, through a Canadian winter, at a cost a health service could afford.

#### Payload: 750 g

Our drone must carry 750 g. This is the gross weight: the samples or medicines, the triple packaging that Canada's dangerous-goods rules require for lab samples, and an insulated box that keeps them from freezing. To choose it, we listed 16 kinds of deliveries a small clinic sends out or receives, estimated the packed weight of each, and scored how often it is needed and how much a faster delivery helps. We then checked what share of that total value each payload size covers.

The biggest single use is the routine daily batch of samples. Canadians have on average 14 to 20 lab tests a year. For a community of 300 to 500 people, assuming about three tests per tube and that most tests are drawn at the local clinic, that comes to roughly 3 to 9 blood tubes per working day, plus one or two urine samples. Packed for winter, a typical day's batch weighs about 0.7 kg: about 100 g of blood tubes, 180 g of urine containers, 100 g of triple packaging and a 350 g insulated box.

The box is the heaviest part. At −30 °C, a small foam box loses about 4 W of heat, so over a one-hour trip it needs about 70 g of phase-change material (a substance that releases heat as it freezes), doubled for safety, plus the foam itself. The samples are only about 40% of what the drone carries. In summer the box can be much lighter, so the same 750 g carries almost twice as many samples.

We sized the drone for this daily run rather than for emergencies. Research on urgent samples shows less benefit than expected: at nurse-run stations in northern Saskatchewan, 89% of chest-pain patients would have been evacuated whatever their blood test showed, and some clinics now have their own point-of-care analyzers. Routine samples are different: they often wait days for transport, and labs reject the ones that arrive too late. In Scotland, drones cut sample transport from 36 hours to 30 minutes. So the drone's main job is a scheduled same-day sample run, with urgent flights as a bonus.

Single urgent items, such as naloxone or one prescription, fit in 300 to 500 g, but a full day's samples only fit at 750 g, which is why the biggest jump comes there. After 750 g the curve flattens: doubling to 1.5 kg adds only about 7 points, while making the drone, its battery and its motors much bigger. Busy days, after weekends or weather delays, can reach about 1.2 kg and are split into two flights. The next items need 1 kg or more and are rarely useful by drone at a nursing station: a defibrillator that takes 30 minutes to arrive is too late, nursing stations generally don't give blood transfusions, and IV fluids are kept in stock. An Oslo University Hospital study found that even a large hospital's lab traffic never needed more than 3.5 kg per flight.

Going below 750 g would cost more than it saves. A 500 g drone could still carry single urgent items, but it would split every day's samples into two flights, and samples make up about 60% of the value in our scoring. The most uncertain number is the insulated box: if it weighs 500 g instead of 350 g, a day's samples no longer fit and we would need about 1 kg, so we will build and cold-test the box first.

**What each payload size adds, packed for winter:**

| **Payload** | **What it adds** | **What still doesn't fit** |
|---|---|---|
| 300 g | Swabs, dried blood spot cards, a naloxone kit, one urgent prescription | A day's samples in a winter box |
| 500 g | An urgent blood sample, a patient's prescriptions, insulin, emergency drugs | The routine daily batch of samples |
| **750 g (our target)** | **A typical day's samples, packed for −30 °C** | **A peak-day batch (about 1.2 kg), which needs two flights** |
| 1 kg | Vaccine shipments, 1 unit of blood | A peak-day batch, IV fluids |
| 1.5 kg | A peak-day batch, small device parts | 2 units of blood, IV fluids |
| 2 kg | 2 units of blood, 1 L of IV fluid | Bulk resupply |
| 3 kg and up | Bulk clinic resupply | — |

#### Range: 95 km one way

Our drone must fly 95 km one way on a single battery, in a straight line between two landing pads, with a fresh battery at each end. To choose this number, we first measured 70 real routes across Canada, then focused on Quebec. We kept only routes that genuinely need a drone, such as a community cut off by a bad road, an unreliable ferry or a seasonal ice crossing, and left out places with a good paved road or daily flights. We then looked at each route on six things: how badly current transport fails, how many people it serves, how much time a drone saves, how easy it is to fly, how much battery margin it leaves, and whether local partners are interested.

The drone does not have to fly all the way to the hospital. It only needs to replace the unreliable part of the trip, then continue after a battery swap or hand off to a road that is already well served, as on the Manawan route through Saint-Michel-des-Saints. This relay approach keeps most useful routes in Quebec under 95 km.

The chart shows how many of these routes each range reaches. Island and river crossings need less than 20 km, and the Lower North Shore links less than 50 km. The big step comes by 75 km, which adds Manawan for example. Going to 95 km adds Wemotaci to the La Tuque hospital (91.5 km): about 1,100 people linked by a 115 km gravel road that was closed by landslides in August 2026. By drone the trip would take about 80 minutes instead of 3.5 to 4 hours by road, fast enough for routine blood samples that must reach the lab within about 2 hours.

Going further brings little, as the lighter bars show. Doubling the range to about 200 km would add only about six more routes, and most of them, such as Whapmagoostui and Chevery, already have scheduled flights; only Opitciwan, at about 144 km, has none. Reaching them would take about twice the range, and for an electric drone that cannot be bought by simply making it bigger: range depends on how much of the drone's weight is battery, and in practice that share tops out around half. Working drone networks have settled on similar distances: Zipline serves about 80 km around each base, and Aviant flies routes of about 120 km.

**What 95 km means in still air**

Still air means no wind relative to the ground; it does not by itself specify temperature or battery condition. Still-air cruise range is a reference distance at a stated airspeed, temperature and payload, using the energy allocated to cruise after accounting separately for take-off, transitions, landing and the required reserve. It is not a delivery radius or a flight-to-empty operating limit. Our mission target remains 95 km one way while carrying 750 g of packaged cargo, with battery swaps at the endpoints. Any quoted range must state its conditions and energy accounting so that usable capacity, aging and reserve are not deducted twice. The table below is an illustrative comparison using preliminary assumptions, not a validated range capability or a selected aircraft-sizing requirement. A real route needs more, because several factors each add energy, and they multiply rather than add. The table works through a 95 km route on a calm, mild day and in an illustrative cold/headwind case, not a qualified operating limit: −30 °C with a 25 km/h headwind.

| **Factor** | **Why it matters** | **Multiplier** | **Calm, mild day** | **Worst winter day** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Straight-line route** | Distance between the two landing pads | — | 95 km | 95 km |
| **Detour** | Avoiding airports, towns and terrain | ×1.10 | 105 km | 105 km |
| **Headwind** | 25 km/h against our 70 km/h cruise; on a one-way leg it can blow the whole way | ×1.56 (winter only) | — | 163 km |
| **Cold, dense air** | Air at −30 °C is about 18% denser, so drag rises | ×1.07 (winter only) | — | 175 km |
| **Cold battery** | Even a pre-heated battery gives about 10% less | ×1.10 (winter only) | — | 193 km |
| **Reserve** | About 20% extra energy to hold or divert | ×1.25 | 131 km | 241 km |
| **Take-off, landing and one go-around** | Hovering uses 5 to 7 times cruise power; about 3 minutes per leg | +17 to 20 km | 148 km | 261 km |
| **Usable battery** | Only 90% of the pack is used, to protect it | ÷0.90 | 164 km | 290 km |
| **Battery aging** | After many cycles a pack keeps about 85% of its capacity | ÷0.85 | 193 km | 341 km |
| **Still-air range needed** |  | **×2.0 and ×3.6 overall** | **about 190 km** | **about 340 km** |

So 95 km on the map means about 190 km of still-air range on a calm day, and about 340 km on the worst winter day. Because the factors multiply, a few modest margins add up to two to three and a half times the route. The headwind is the largest, because on a one-way trip it can blow against the drone the whole way; flying faster helps, since at 100 km/h the same wind costs ×1.34 instead of ×1.56. An electric drone our size cannot reach 340 km, because range depends on how much of its weight is battery, and that share tops out around half. These illustrative equivalents do not set a new range target or establish the aircraft size. We will size the scaled aircraft for the 95 km one-way mission with the 750 g packaged payload under explicitly defined conditions and reserve requirements. Wind and temperature limits will be established separately; long legs will wait for suitable weather or use an appropriate battery-swap stop, as proposed for the Manawan route. All the multipliers are our estimates, and our first flight tests will measure the real energy use.

### Our design: a 4+1 quadplane with a V-tail

Before choosing a specific aircraft, we chose a layout. There are three common ways to build a VTOL fixed-wing drone, and they differ in what has to turn 90° between taking off and cruising. We chose the one where nothing turns: a 4+1 quadplane with a V-tail.

#### Three ways to take off vertically

A tilt-rotor, such as the Wingcopter 198, tilts its motors from pointing up for take-off to pointing forward for cruise. A tailsitter, such as the WingtraOne, stands on its tail and tips the whole aircraft 90° to fly forward. A quadplane keeps two separate sets of motors: four that point up and are used only for take-off and landing, and one pusher at the back that drives it forward. Nothing turns, and the aircraft stays level like an airplane from take-off to landing.

| **Layout** | **What turns 90°** | **Main strength** | **Main drawback for us** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tilt-rotor** | The motors | Uses the same motors to hover and to cruise, so it carries less extra weight | Tilting mechanisms are extra moving parts that can jam or stiffen in the cold, and a fault mid-tilt is hard to recover from |
| **Tailsitter** | The whole aircraft | No extra motors or tilting parts | Standing on its tail, its wing catches gusts during take-off and landing, and the cargo turns on its side |
| **Quadplane (our choice)** | Nothing | Simple and level, with two independent motor systems | The lift motors and their arms are extra weight and drag during cruise |

#### How a flight works

Our drone rises straight up on its four lift motors, like a quadcopter. Once it is clear of obstacles, the pusher speeds it up until the wing carries its weight, and the lift motors switch off. It then cruises as a normal airplane. Near the clinic it slows down, the lift motors start again, and it lands straight down on a small pad. The tail is a V-tail: two angled surfaces instead of the usual three, whose movable edges the flight controller uses both to pitch up or down and to turn. It has fewer parts and joints than a regular tail, so it is lighter and quicker to print and build.

#### Why this layout fits our missions

Simplicity is the main reason. Our drone has to fly in winter, far from help and beyond the pilot's sight. A quadplane has no tilting mechanism: its only moving parts are the motors and the control surfaces any airplane has, so fewer things can freeze, jam or wear out. It also has two independent ways to stay in the air. If the pusher fails, the lift motors can bring the drone down vertically like a quadcopter, and if it slows down too much in cruise, the flight controller can switch the lift motors back on automatically. A lift motor that fails in cruise is not needed until landing. This directly supports our safety criterion of landing safely after a fault.

Because each set of motors does one job, each can be chosen for it: the lift motors for short, strong bursts of thrust, and the pusher for efficient cruising at 70 km/h or more. That helps our range and speed targets. The price is weight and drag: the lift motors and their arms are carried the whole way but used for only a minute or two on each leg. A tilt-rotor avoids part of this cost, but we accept it in exchange for a simpler, safer aircraft. Because the drone stays level, so does its cargo: the samples ride upright as they would in a car, and the 750 g box can sit at the balance point under the wing.

At the clinic, a level aircraft is also easier to land on a small pad in gusty wind than a tailsitter standing on its tail, whose wing catches the wind like a sail. Finally, this layout is the easiest to build and fly. The motors are fixed in place, most of the airframe can be 3D-printed, and ArduPilot, the free open-source autopilot, supports the 4+1 quadplane directly, with years of use by other builders. That helps keep the parts under our CA$3,500 target. Professional drones such as the DeltaQuad use the same layout.

#### Our starting point: the Flightory Stork

Instead of designing an airframe from scratch, we are starting from the Stork, a 3D-printable drone by Flightory, a Polish designer of printed aircraft. The Stork already has our layout: a pusher motor and a V-tail, with an optional VTOL kit that adds four lift motors on carbon booms and landing skids, turning it into a 4+1 quadplane. It is printed mostly in lightweight foaming PLA, with stronger PC or PETG parts and carbon tubes where loads are high, and every part fits on a common 220 mm printer bed.

Highlighted rows are from Flightony, a third-party vendor's build of the Stork VTOL, not from Flightory, the designer. Its other specs match Flightory's. Flightony's figures assume optimal conditions and appear calculated rather than flight-tested.

#### Where the stock Stork falls short

Measured against our success criteria, the stock Stork meets our layout, vertical landing, printing and cost goals, but falls short on everything that sets the size of the mission.

| **Criterion** | **Our target** | **Stock Stork VTOL** | **Verdict** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Payload** | 750 g, packed | 200 g at full range; 500 g with a smaller battery | Short |
| **Still-air range** | About 190 km carrying 750 g | 104 to 130 km carrying 200 g, best case | Short |
| **Cruise speed** | At least 70 km/h | 54 to 60 km/h for best efficiency | Short |
| **Payload bay** | Fits an insulated sample box | 205 × 65 × 80 mm, shared with electronics | Too small |
| **Winter operation** | Down to −30 °C with warmed batteries | No cold-weather data | Unknown |
| **Take-off and landing** | Vertical, from a small pad | Vertical, landing skids | Met |
| **Build and cost** | 3D-printed, parts under about CA$3,500 | Fully printable with hobby parts | Met |

These gaps come from the Stork's size, not from a flaw in its design. Flightony's 1.2 kg battery and 200 g of cargo already bring it to its 3.1 kg maximum weight, so carrying 750 g means taking out battery, and the available cruise energy and loaded range are reduced; the stock aircraft has not demonstrated our simultaneous 95 km one-way and 750 g packaged-payload requirement. Its wing is sized to be most efficient at about 55 to 60 km/h, so cruising at 70 km/h costs more energy per kilometre. Slow cruising also makes wind worse: a 25 km/h headwind multiplies the energy for a trip by about 1.8 at 57 km/h, compared with 1.56 at our 70 km/h. And a bay 65 mm wide leaves no room for the insulation a winter sample box needs.

#### Our conclusion: scale up the Stork

No small change fixes all of these at once, because every fix needs more weight, and the Stork has none to spare. We will therefore keep what makes the Stork a good starting point, its layout, wing shape, airfoil and printed construction, and make it bigger. Scaling allows more room for the packaged payload and battery, but the battery mass fraction and resulting performance remain to be determined from the complete aircraft mass and energy budgets. A bigger wing can also be shaped to be most efficient at 70 km/h or more instead of 55 to 60 km/h, and a larger fuselage gives room for an insulated sample box. Scaling up is a firm design decision, but the scale factor, wingspan and take-off mass have not yet been selected. They will be determined from the simultaneous 95 km one-way and 750 g packaged-payload requirements, together with the mass and energy budgets, structural design and manufacturing constraints, and then validated incrementally. We are not setting a numerical size or mass starting point at this stage. The enlarged parts will be divided into pieces that still fit a common printer.

### Competitors around the world

These are the companies abroad whose drones or missions are closest to ours.

**Aviant**, in Norway, was founded in 2020 by three students from NTNU, a Norwegian university, who met on exchange at MIT. Its electric fixed-wing VTOL drones have a 2.6 m wingspan and, depending on the model, carry 1 to 3 kg, compared with our 750 g. In March 2021 it flew hospital blood samples 120 km between Røros and Trondheim. Aviant reports flying in winds up to 18 m/s and at −26 °C, but weather is still a limit: in a home-care pilot in late 2024, 46 flights were flown and 38 cancelled because of wind, fog, icing or lost mobile signal. With little steady health funding, Aviant has turned largely to home delivery of food and, since 2026, pharmacy orders. Its next hospital trials are planned for 2027 and 2028.

**Zipline**, in the United States, is the world's largest medical drone operator. Since its first deliveries in Rwanda in 2016, it has passed 2 million deliveries, most of them health supplies in Africa. Its P1 is an electric fixed-wing drone launched by catapult. It drops packages of about 1.8 kg by parachute anywhere within about 80 km of its base (our target is 95 km one way), then flies home and is caught by a wire, so it cannot land at a clinic or collect samples. Zipline depends on governments to pay: in November 2025 it suspended three centres in Ghana over about US$15 million in unpaid bills.

**Wingcopter**, in Germany, builds the Wingcopter 198, an electric VTOL whose propellers tilt forward so it can fly like an airplane. It carries up to 4.7 kg, about six times our payload, and has a maximum range of about 94 km, less with a full load, and its 2021 spec sheet rates it down to only 0 °C. In Malawi it made more than 2,150 delivery flights to remote health centres, showing that a VTOL drone can serve rural clinics daily. Malawi's medical drone deliveries have largely stopped since donor funding was cut, and in 2026 Wingcopter added a defence business.

**Avy**, in the Netherlands, builds the Aera, an electric drone with the same layout we plan: four lifting propellers and one pushing propeller. At 19.5 kg maximum take-off weight, it is much larger than ours. It carries up to 3 kg (2 kg with its parachute) about 80 to 100 km, in a medical box that stays between 2 and 8 °C for about seven hours, and it is rated down to only 0 °C. Avy holds a Dutch licence to fly medical routes beyond the pilot's sight.

**DeltaQuad**, also in the Netherlands, makes the Pro #CARGO, an electric quadplane that carries 1.2 kg about 100 km, is rated down to −20 °C and is listed from about €10,000. It is the closest drone to our 750 g and 95 km targets, and DeltaQuad says Aviant used a DeltaQuad Pro for its first 120 km hospital flight. It sells aircraft, not a service, so buyers must run and pay for the flights.

**Delivrone**, a start-up from Normandy, France, flew lab samples between hospitals in the Somme region from late 2024 to early 2025, on routes of about 80 km. It used the Eiger, an electric VTOL drone from the Swiss company Rigitech that carries 3 kg up to 100 km (about 80 km at full load). The Eiger is rated down to 0 °C and has been tested at −7 °C. Researchers found it safe for the samples but no faster than by road, and said backup transport is essential in bad weather.

### Competitors in Quebec and Canada

No drone service delivers medical supplies on a regular schedule in Quebec today. Elsewhere in Canada, the regular services we found fly short routes.

**CORRIDAIR**, a project of the Quebec company Vertiko Mobilité and Jaunt Air Mobility, is the corridor described in case study 2: about 150 km between the Joliette hospital and Manawan. The Manawan band council backed it in 2023, Quebec granted it up to $2 million in 2024, and its partners include Thales, CAE and Drones Express. Deliveries were planned for 2026, but as of October 2026 we found no report of any flights, and Jaunt is now developing air taxis and a cargo drone carrying 110 to 230 kg, over 100 times our 750 g payload.

**ECOGENE-21** is a Quebec non-profit clinical research organization based in Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean. In 2022 it ran medication delivery demonstrations with a small drone at the Alma drone centre. In January 2024, with the French company Delair, it flew a DT46 electric VTOL fixed-wing drone in loops totalling more than 150 km over Delair's test range near Toulouse, France, carrying a temperature-controlled case of medicines and blood samples. The DT46 weighs up to 25 kg, carries up to 5 kg and is rated down to −10 °C. It was a one-time demonstration with no Quebec flights since; in 2024, ECOGENE-21 said its next step was finding drone operators to work with.

**Drones Express**, based in the Quebec City area, flew simulated medical parcels in August 2024 between the Baie-Saint-Paul hospital and L'Isle-aux-Coudres, about 6 km each way and mostly beyond the pilot's sight, using an octocopter that carries up to 11 kg. The tests, approved by the MRC de Charlevoix, succeeded, but the company said the regional health authority lacked the resources to take part in the next step, and no results from a promised follow-up pilot have been published. Its 2020 plan to fly large fuel-powered drones on the Lower North Shore never launched.

**Volatus Aerospace**, which merged with Drone Delivery Canada in 2024 and now has its headquarters in Mirabel, Quebec, runs the only regular medical drone routes we found in Canada. Its Canary multicopter carries 4.5 kg up to 20 km, about a fifth of our 95 km target, and has been tested at −35 °C. It flies just over 10 km from Edmonton's airport to the Montana First Nation clinic, and about 14 km between Halton Healthcare hospitals in Milton and Oakville, carrying blood and urine samples. Earlier projects went less well: a 2018 service planned with Moose Cree First Nation in Ontario stalled while funding was sought and never started, and the large fuel-powered Condor drone was cancelled in late 2023, months after a test crash destroyed one.

**The UBC Drone Transport Initiative** is a University of British Columbia research program run with First Nations partners. From October 2021 to October 2022, it made 1,228 flights on a 5 km route between Fraser Lake and Stellat'en First Nation, with drones operated by Drone Delivery Canada and funding mainly from a $750,000 TD grant. Those flights included pharmacy deliveries; lab sample transport was only planned. In 2026 it began a second phase with InDro Robotics, which plans longer fixed-wing VTOL flights from Prince George and is evaluating the SkyLane 350, a 35 kg drone that carries up to 10 kg and is rated down to −20 °C.

### Takeaways

#### What we learned

Most projects we studied stopped because the money ran out, not because the drones failed: Malawi's drone deliveries ended with donor funding, Volatus's Moose Cree service was never funded, Zipline suspended centres in Ghana over unpaid bills, and Drones Express stalled when, it says, the health authority lacked resources. A service only lasts if a health system agrees to pay for it from the start.

Reliability is the other lesson: in one Aviant pilot, 38 of 84 planned flights were cancelled for wind, fog, icing or lost signal, and Delivrone's researchers said backup transport is essential in bad weather. Landing matters too: Zipline's P1 never lands, so it cannot pick up samples.

#### Where the field is crowded, and where it isn't

Short hops of 5 to 15 km, like those of Volatus and Drones Express, are already proven, and so are large national networks in warm countries, like Zipline's. Almost no one flies small loads 50 to 100 km to small clinics on a regular schedule through a Canadian winter; UBC's second phase plans to try. Quebec has no regular medical drone service at all.

#### How we are similar and different

QxF7 is closest to Aviant: like them, we are students building an electric fixed-wing VTOL drone for long medical routes in a cold climate. It has the same layout as Avy's Aera, a size and range close to DeltaQuad's Pro #CARGO, and serves the same kind of communities as CORRIDAIR and UBC.

We differ in three ways. Our drone is designed to be smaller and much cheaper: it is 3D printed and built around a 750 g load, a batch of samples rather than a large parcel. It is built for lab samples on a fixed schedule, landing at the clinic in both directions. And it is designed for an ordinary Canadian winter. Temperatures of −30 °C are uncommon where we plan to fly, so our goal is not a cold record but dependable operation within a defined and validated cold-weather envelope. Cold-temperature capability is not icing capability: flight in known or forecast icing conditions is excluded from the current project scope. Wind, precipitation, visibility and battery-temperature limits will be assessed separately, and missions will wait or use backup transport when conditions are outside the approved operating envelope. Every drone here with a published cold rating stops between 0 and −20 °C.

#### Why our idea is viable

The need is documented and the technology has been proven: in 2021 Aviant flew hospital samples 120 km with a small electric VTOL drone, and it has since flown at −26 °C. Our work is making this cheaper and reliable enough for Canadian winters. Since November 2025, Transport Canada lets certified pilots and operators fly some lower-risk routes beyond sight without a special permit, but only away from towns and airports, so our clinic landings will need extra approval, as will carrying lab samples, which count as dangerous goods.

People, insurance and approvals cost more than the drone, so we will not work alone: after short test routes, we will work with communities, health authorities and projects like CORRIDAIR so that a service can be paid for and last.
